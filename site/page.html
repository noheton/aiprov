aiprov

AI provenance, recorded, open, verifiable

$doi_badgew3idw3id.org/aiprov/ns

When an AI model drafts your code or your text, two questions come later: who did what, and who checked it? aiprov writes the answers into a plain Turtle file next to your work, as a profile of W3C PROV-O.

  • RecordedEvery step names its agent, person or model, its inputs and outputs by hash, and the telemetry the tool reports. Nothing is estimated.
  • OpenOne Turtle file and one dependency (rdflib). Any tool that reads PROV-O reads the graph.
  • VerifiableEvery statement is a claim on a seven-step ladder. The top two steps belong to people: the command line refuses an AI, and provlog validate checks it again in CI.

A demo: one report, two claims, one human check

Alice asks an AI model to turn her benchmark notes into a short report. Nine commands later, the graph below holds the whole story. The data is invented, the graph is real: this page ran the commands with aiprov $version when it was built.

  1. Who. Alice is a person, registered with her ORCID iD. Model X is an AI model that acts on her behalf (prov:actedOnBehalfOf).
  2. What. Model X drafted report.md from notes.md. The activity records the model, both files (the output with its sha256) and the tokens the tool reported: 1,200 in, 800 out.
  3. Claims. The report makes two statements. Each becomes a claim with a parent activity and a state on the ladder: "twice as fast" was checked by the model (AI-confirmed), "all functions tested" by nobody yet (unverified).
  4. Human check. Alice re-ran the benchmark and promoted the speed claim to human-confirmed. The promotion is an activity of its own, an audit pass associated with Alice, so the graph says who granted the step and when.
  5. The rule. Model X then tried to mark the other claim as human-read. provlog refused: the top two steps are for registered humans. validate checks the same rule on the whole file, so a hand-edited graph cannot slip it in either.
